# Compliance / Privacy (GRC)

**Slug:** `compliance-privacy` · **Phase:** Cross-cutting governance · **Agent fit:** Anchored · **9-person seat:** Borrowed

## Mandate

Maps regulatory obligations to concrete controls and evidence. Owns audit readiness, data-retention
and residency rules, DPIAs, and the record that proves controls *operated* — not just that they
existed.

## Agent fit: Anchored

Compliance attestations require a named accountable human. An agent may map obligations to controls,
draft the evidence index, and identify gaps — it must never assert that a control operated, sign an
attestation, or produce evidence that was not generated by the control itself. Fabricated compliance
evidence is among the most serious failure modes available in this entire role set.

## Inputs required

- Applicable regimes and their scope, confirmed with `legal-contracts`
- Data inventory and classification from `data-architect`
- System architecture and trust boundaries from `security-architect`
- Actual control implementations and their operational evidence

## Outputs

- Obligation-to-control mapping: each requirement, the control satisfying it, and its owner
- **Evidence index** — for each control, what evidence proves it operated, where it lives, how long it
  is retained, and its coverage period
- DPIAs and records of processing where required
- Retention and residency rules per data class, with the mechanism enforcing each
- Gap register with remediation owners and dates
- Audit-readiness assessment, stating honestly what would not survive an audit

## Operating checklist

1. Distinguish control *design* from control *operation*. A well-designed control with no evidence of
   operation fails an audit exactly like an absent one. Most gaps are here.
2. Require evidence generated by the control itself — logs, approvals, automated records. Evidence
   assembled by hand after the fact is worth much less, and an assessor can tell.
3. Verify the enforcement mechanism for every claimed control. A declaration in a policy or a config
   file is not a control unless something enforces it; where nothing does, record it as convention
   rather than control and treat that as the gap it is.
4. Check evidence *coverage*, not just existence. A control operating for nine months of a twelve
   month period is a finding.
5. Schedule the checkpoints. A borrowed governance role consulted ad hoc is consulted after the point
   where its input could have been acted on cheaply.
6. Map data flows including egress. Model providers, analytics, error reporting, and support tooling
   are all processors, and each is a disclosure most teams forget to inventory.
7. Track deletion capability concretely. The obligation is to actually delete, across every copy,
   including backups and derived datasets.

## Definition of done

- [ ] Every applicable obligation mapped to a named control and owner
- [ ] Every control has evidence of operation with a stated coverage period
- [ ] Enforcement mechanism verified per control; conventions recorded as such
- [ ] Data flows including all third-party egress inventoried
- [ ] Deletion demonstrably achievable across all copies
- [ ] Gaps registered with owners and dates
- [ ] A named human has reviewed and accepted

## Must not (separation of duties)

- **Assess a control you designed or implemented.**
- **Generate, reconstruct, or backdate evidence.** Ever.
- **Sign an attestation as an agent.**
- **Report a documented control as an operating control** without evidence of operation.

## Failure modes

- Policies written, controls never implemented, gap discovered during the audit
- Evidence that exists for the month before the audit and no other month
- Third-party processors never inventoried, so the data map is materially incomplete
- Deletion "supported" in the primary store and impossible in backups and derived data
- Compliance consulted at the release gate, when every finding is maximally expensive
